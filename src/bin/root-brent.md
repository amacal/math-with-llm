# Brent's Method for Root-Finding

## Overview

Two root-finders already sit in this repository at opposite ends of a tradeoff. Bisection, covered in `mod-isqrt-bisect.md`, is unconditionally safe: given two points where a continuous function has opposite signs, it is guaranteed to converge no matter how badly the function behaves, but it only ever halves its search interval, a linear rate. The secant method, covered in `root-secant.md`, is fast: its error shrinks with order equal to the golden ratio, roughly 1.618, but it carries no safety guarantee at all and can diverge or cycle forever, exactly as Newton's method was shown to do on f(x) = x^3 - x. Brent's method asks whether an algorithm can have both properties at once, by running a faster method, inverse quadratic interpolation, whenever it can be trusted, and always keeping bisection available as a fallback the moment that trust is violated. This session derives inverse quadratic interpolation from first principles, derives the two safeguard conditions that decide when to trust it, and argues why the resulting hybrid can never do worse than plain bisection in the worst case; because it genuinely synthesizes three previously separate algorithms into one, this write-up runs somewhat longer than a single-concept session.

## Deriving inverse quadratic interpolation

Secant fits a straight line through two points and evaluates where that line crosses zero. With a third point available, the natural extension is to fit a parabola through all three and evaluate where the parabola crosses zero instead. Fitting a normal parabola y = f(x) and solving f(x) = 0 for x, however, reintroduces the quadratic formula's two candidate roots, with no principled way to choose between them. The fix is to flip which variable is being fitted: build x as a function of y instead, a parabola opening sideways, so that finding "where y is zero" becomes a single, unambiguous evaluation of that function at y = 0, rather than solving an equation.

Building this function uses the classical Lagrange construction. Given three points t0, t1, t2 with desired outputs v0, v1, v2, a "switch" function for point i is built so that it equals 1 at t_i and 0 at every other t_j:

$$L_i(t) = \frac{\prod_{j \neq i}(t - t_j)}{\prod_{j \neq i}(t_i - t_j)}$$

The numerator forces the zeros, since each factor (t - t_j) vanishes whenever t lands on some other point t_j, while the denominator is the same product evaluated at t_i itself, normalizing the whole expression to exactly 1 there. Summing g(t) = v0 L0(t) + v1 L1(t) + v2 L2(t) then reproduces every one of the three desired outputs exactly, because at t = t_i every switch except L_i vanishes, leaving g(t_i) = v_i.

Applying this to Brent's setting, the roles of "input" and "output" are inverted from the usual picture: the inputs are the three function values y0, y1, y2 (where y_i = f(x_i)), and the outputs are the corresponding x-values. Substituting these into the general switch formula and evaluating the whole sum at y = 0, since that is the input value corresponding to a root, gives the closed-form update rule

$$x_{\text{new}} = x_0\,\frac{y_1 y_2}{(y_0-y_1)(y_0-y_2)} \;+\; x_1\,\frac{y_0 y_2}{(y_1-y_0)(y_1-y_2)} \;+\; x_2\,\frac{y_0 y_1}{(y_2-y_0)(y_2-y_1)}$$

which is inverse quadratic interpolation: "inverse" because x is fitted as a function of y, not the other way around. Its order of convergence, approximately 1.84, is higher than secant's golden ratio, since it uses one extra point of curvature information beyond secant's straight line.

## The two safeguard conditions

Fast interpolation of this kind, used on its own, inherits secant's complete lack of a safety guarantee: nothing in the formula above prevents x_new from landing anywhere at all, including entirely outside whatever bracket [lo, hi] is currently known to contain a root. Brent's method restores safety by checking the proposed x_new against two conditions before accepting it, and falling back to the ordinary bisection midpoint (lo + hi)/2 whenever either one fails.

The first condition is geometric: if x_new falls outside [lo, hi], it cannot be trusted, since accepting it would break the one invariant the whole algorithm depends on. The second is about the rate of progress rather than location: even a step that stays safely inside the bracket could still shrink it by a vanishingly small amount, iteration after iteration, without ever making genuine progress. The check compares the size of the proposed new step to half the size of the step taken two iterations earlier, rejecting the step if it is not at least that much smaller. The constant one half here is not an arbitrary tuning knob; it is exactly bisection's own guaranteed shrink factor, so the check is really asking whether this fast step is at least as good as bisection would have guaranteed anyway. A third, more mundane failure mode is a degenerate denominator in the interpolation formula, when two of the three tracked points happen to share the same y-value; rather than aborting outright, the implementation folds this into the same fallback mechanism, since a valid bracket is still available and there is no reason to give up entirely over one iteration's broken arithmetic.

## Correctness

The invariant maintained throughout is that the current bracket [lo, hi] always satisfies opposite signs, f(lo) and f(hi) landing on either side of zero. By the same continuity argument built for bisection's own correctness proof, tracing the graph of f continuously from below the x-axis to above it forces a crossing somewhere in between, so this invariant alone guarantees a genuine root is trapped inside the bracket at every point in the algorithm's execution, never merely at the end. Whenever a candidate point, whether from IQI, secant, or the bisection fallback, is accepted, it replaces whichever of lo or hi shares its sign, leaving the remaining pair with opposite signs still, so the invariant survives every iteration by induction. If the original bracket happens to contain several roots rather than one, this same argument still guarantees convergence to some genuine root of f, though not necessarily a predictable one, since nothing in the invariant distinguishes which root remains trapped as the bracket narrows.

Convergence itself follows from tying the safeguard directly to bisection's already-proven guarantee. Bisection alone was shown, in `mod-isqrt-bisect.md`, to sandwich lo and hi down to a single point through an unconditional halving of the bracket's width every iteration. Since any accepted fast step is checked to make at least that same rate of progress, and any step failing this check is replaced by an actual bisection step achieving the guaranteed halving exactly, the bracket's width can never shrink slower, across the run as a whole, than plain bisection's own proven rate. Combined with the invariant above, find_root either converges to a genuine root or, if no bracket with the required sign-change property could be verified from the caller's inputs, correctly reports that failure rather than searching blindly.

One structural limitation follows directly from the sign-change requirement: an even-multiplicity root, such as the double root of f(x) = (x - 1)^2 at x = 1, can never be bracketed at all, because that function is nonnegative everywhere and never actually changes sign, even though the root sits numerically between any two points straddling x = 1. This is not a bug but a direct consequence of relying on a sign change as the entire basis for the safety guarantee; Newton's and secant's methods, which need no bracket at all, can still limp toward such a root numerically, just without their usual superlinear guarantees.

## Complexity

In the worst case, the argument above pins the iteration count to the same bound already derived for bisection, O(log((hi - lo)/eps)), since the safeguard guarantees the bracket-shrinking rate is never worse than bisection's own. In the typical case, when IQI is accepted at nearly every iteration, the same chained-error-recurrence argument used for secant in `root-secant.md` applies unchanged, since that derivation never depended on the specific numeric value of the convergence order, only on it being some constant p greater than one. Substituting IQI's order, approximately 1.84, in place of secant's golden ratio gives the same complexity class, O(log log(1/eps)), just with a smaller constant in front, since 1/log(1.84) is smaller than 1/log(1.618). Brent's method is therefore never worse than bisection asymptotically, and typically matches Newton's and secant's iteration count class in practice.

## Edge cases

A bracket failing the initial opposite-sign check, or one where f is already exactly zero at an endpoint, is handled before any iteration begins: the latter returns that endpoint immediately as a genuine root, while the former returns None, since no bracket with the required property could be verified. The double-root case discussed above under Correctness is, in code, indistinguishable from this same precondition failure: f(x) = (x - 1)^2 on any bracket not landing exactly on x = 1 exercises the identical same-sign rejection path as any other poorly-chosen bracket, rather than some distinct piece of double-root-specific logic. The pathological point near 1/sqrt(5) that trapped Newton's method in an eternal two-cycle, and that secant escaped only after several chaotic-looking steps, has no meaningful analog here: that pathology depended on two points close enough together that the secant slope between them approximated a tangent line, reproducing Newton's derivative-driven dynamics by proxy. Brent's method requires genuinely separated bracket endpoints from the start and never computes anything derivative-like, so the mechanism that produced the trap has nothing to attach to.

## Worked example

Trace the first iteration on f(x) = x^3 - 2 with starting bracket lo = 1, hi = 2, the same function and values used in `root-secant.md`'s own worked example. First, f(1) = -1 and f(2) = 6, opposite signs, so the precondition holds and a root is trapped somewhere inside. With only these two points available, the first candidate comes from the plain secant formula: the slope is (6 - (-1))/(2 - 1) = 7, and the update gives x_new = 2 - 6/7, approximately 1.143. This sits safely inside [1, 2], surviving the outside-bracket test; the insufficient-progress test has no prior step to compare against yet, so it is skipped this once. Evaluating f(1.143) gives approximately -0.507, negative, the same sign as f(1). By the bracket-update rule, lo is replaced by 1.143 while hi stays at 2, and the new bracket [1.143, 2] still satisfies f(1.143) negative and f(2) positive, preserving the invariant. From here, with three points now available (the current lo, hi, and the displaced former lo = 1, kept as the extra interpolation point), subsequent iterations switch to the full inverse quadratic interpolation formula.
